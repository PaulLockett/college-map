The short answer first

College and law school are two different things.

College comes first. It's four years, and you can study almost anything you want — art, biology, music, history. Nobody studies law yet.

Law school comes after that. It's three more years, and it's a completely separate school you apply to once college is done. That's where you actually learn law.

So: you don't pick between them. You do one, then the other.

Wait — what even is college?

It's a school you live at.Most people move there. You get a dorm room, a roommate, and a cafeteria. Your parents aren't down the hall.
You build your own schedule.No one hands you a timetable. You pick your classes and what time of day they happen. Some days you have one class. Some days you have four.
You pick a "major."That's the one subject you go deep on — maybe a third of your classes. The rest you choose freely.
There's no homework-for-points.Grades come from a few big things: papers, projects, exams. That's a real adjustment — and it's why the reading habit you build in high school matters more than the homework does.
It takes about four years.Freshman, sophomore, junior, senior — same words as high school, done again.

The whole path, start to finish

Show me the years

This is the stage where the doors open or quietly close, and almost none of it is about law.

  • Your grades across all four years become one number colleges look at. Freshman year counts.
  • Take the hardest classes you can handle. Honors, AP, dual enrollment. A B in a hard class beats an A in an easy one, and it's the single best thing you can do this year.
  • Read a lot, on purpose. Every test between here and becoming a lawyer is secretly a reading-speed-and-comprehension test. Manga counts less than novels. Novels count less than arguments you disagree with.
  • Junior year you'll take the ACT or SAT — one test, taken a few times, that colleges use.
  • Keep doing the thing you're already obsessed with. Depth in one weird area beats being vaguely fine at nine.
Whatever you're already into isn't a detour. Sticking with one hard thing for years — a language, an instrument, a sport, code — is exactly what college applications, law schools, and law firms are all hunting for evidence of. It does not have to look like law to count.

You apply during senior year of high school and pick from wherever says yes.

  • You do not have to major in anything law-related. This surprises everybody. Future lawyers major in English, philosophy, history, economics, engineering, art — all of it works. Law schools genuinely don't care which one.
  • What they do care about is your college GPA. It's one of the two numbers that decides where you get in.
  • You'd probably join things — a debate team, a student newspaper, a legal-aid volunteer group — because that's where you find out whether you actually like arguing for a living.
  • Summers become internships. Some of them at actual law offices.
Pick a major you'd enjoy even if you never became a lawyer. You'll do better in it, your GPA will be higher, and a higher GPA is what gets you into law school. The "useful-sounding" major is a trap.

It's the law school version of the ACT. Scored 120 to 180.

  • Here's the strange part: there is no law on it. None. You haven't studied any law yet, and that's normal.
  • It's a reasoning test — reading dense passages, finding the hole in someone's argument, spotting what a claim quietly assumes.
  • It used to have logic puzzles. Those were removed in 2024. Now it's two logical reasoning sections plus reading comprehension, plus a separate essay you write beforehand.
  • Your LSAT score and your college GPA are basically the two numbers. Together they decide most of it.
This is the one that's genuinely trainable. People move their scores a lot with real practice. It rewards exactly the skill you'd be building all through high school: reading something hard and not getting fooled by it.

Three years. You come out with a degree called a JD. This is the first time you study law at all.

  • First year is famously brutal. Contracts, torts, criminal law, constitutional law. Professors call on you without warning and ask you to defend a position in front of the room.
  • You read actual court decisions — real fights between real people — and work out why the judge ruled that way.
  • You do mock trials and moot court, which is exactly what it sounds like: practice arguing.
  • Second and third year you pick a lane — criminal, immigration, corporate, environmental, international — and start working real cases through legal clinics and summer jobs at firms.
This is where "lawyer" stops being one job. A public defender and a corporate lawyer both went here and their days look nothing alike. You don't have to know which one you want until you're standing in it.

Finishing law school doesn't make you a lawyer. Passing the bar does. It's a license, like a driver's license — and it's per-state.

  • The exam was just redesigned. The new version launched in July 2026 — digital, about nine hours over a day and a half, and more about doing the job than memorizing everything cold.
  • Most states will have switched to it by 2028, so it's the one you'd take.
  • Results take a couple of months. Then you get sworn in — there's a ceremony — and you're a lawyer.

You can sign your name on legal documents, stand up in a courtroom, and give advice people are allowed to rely on.

And you'd get there in your mid-twenties — which is younger than most people assume when they hear "eleven years."

Things people get wrong

You have to major in "pre-law."

Major in whatever you like most. Law schools accept every major and don't prefer any of them.

Law school is part of college.

Separate school, separate application, separate campus sometimes. You finish one, then apply to the other.

You need to know what kind of lawyer you'll be.

Most people figure that out in their second year of law school — that's roughly nine years from now.

Lawyers are in court all day.

Most lawyers rarely see a courtroom. Most of the job is reading, writing, and explaining things to people who are stressed.

If you don't start now you've missed it.

Nothing on this list can be started before college except grades and reading. That's genuinely all there is to do right now.

So what do you actually do this year?

Three things. That's the whole list.

Everything above happens later. Here's the part that's yours right now:

  1. Get into the hardest classes they'll let you into. Ask your counselor in the first month — placement gets locked in early, and it sets the ceiling on everything after.
  2. Read things that are harder than you're comfortable with. Twenty minutes a day. This is the actual skill the LSAT tests in eight years.
  3. Keep going with the hard thing you already started. Not because it's required — because sticking with something for years is the rarest thing on any application, and the only way to have done it for years is to not stop now.
